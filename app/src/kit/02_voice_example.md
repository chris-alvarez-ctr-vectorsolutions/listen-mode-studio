# Voice example

Written by hand by Chris Alvarez. This is the tone every script matches. Imitate its moves and register, not its exact lines.

## Cold open

DANA: Today, on the show we're talking about sharps: what they are, how to handle them, and the dangers of not following procedure.
DANA: I'm Dana...
RAY: And I'm Ray.
DANA: Let's get into it.

## Close

DANA: Well, that's it for today. Thank you for listening!
RAY: Keep learning, stay safe, and we'll be back soon.

## K1 (Base)

Beats, written before the dialogue:
- Introduce Ray as co-host and mention his background.
- Introduce or reinforce the concept of sharps: basic definition; not just needles (diabetes, allergies).
- Common shortcuts.
- The right approach.
- Final points.

DANA: So Ray, you spent your whole career ensuring safety in the workplace, is that right?
RAY: Yeah, implementing and enforcing safety policies in various roles and a few different companies.
DANA: So, what would you say is one of the most common safety hazards you've seen people ignore?
RAY: Well, it's a small word, but it has a big impact and there are hundreds of injuries related to it every year.
DANA: So what is it?
RAY: Sharps.
DANA: Sharps. Ok. So what is a sharp?
RAY: So, usually when people think of a sharp, they think of a needle, but a sharp can be anything that is capable of piercing the skin: nails, knives, blades, broken glass, metal... you get the idea.
DANA: Ah so that sounds like the hard part: remembering that it's not just needles, it's anything that could puncture the skin.
RAY: Exactly. And keep in mind you can still find needles in the workplace. Some people need to manage their diabetes or allergies with syringes.
DANA: Let's look why sharps are so dangerous. What is one of the most common, and perhaps most preventable, shortcuts workers take that result in an injury?
RAY: One of the most common shortcuts is simply leaving a blade in the open. Every shift there's someone who leaves a box-cutter blade on a bench thinking "I'll take care of it in a minute." But, a minute is all it takes for an injury.
DANA: So what should someone do instead in this situation? There's an old blade in your hand, and you need to get back to work...
RAY: You have to decide before the blade ever comes out. Four things: One? Plan where it's going before you start. Two: If there's a safer tool for the job, use it. Three: If it has a safety feature, use it. And four, when you're done, it goes straight into a designated sharps container.
DANA: So not on the bench?
RAY: Not the bench. Not in the trash, and don't recap it, bend it, or try to break it.
DANA: Any final thoughts on the dangers of sharps?
RAY: I always tell teams that the second a sharp leaves your hand, it puts everyone else at risk. The risk is yours to prevent.
DANA: Solid advice, Ray.

## What to imitate

- The guest confirms his own background in his own words.
- Tease, then reveal: Ray hints, Dana asks, Ray names it in one word.
- One concrete image of the shortcut, with the words people actually say ("I'll take care of it in a minute").
- Dana paraphrases the key insight once, adding a frame ("that sounds like the hard part").
- Ray gives the procedure as numbered steps.
- Ray speaks from experience ("I always tell teams...").
- Plain, warm register. Conversational connectors: "So," "Ah," "Exactly," "you get the idea."

## Bad, then better

A generated cold open that missed, and why.

**Bad**

DANA: End of a shift. Somebody grabs a full trash bag by the knot and lifts it off the floor.
DANA: That's the whole moment. Hold onto that picture.
DANA: Today is for anyone on a production floor who works around used blades and needles.
DANA: We'll look at a hazard you can walk right past. Why a container does more than a pair of gloves. And what most people actually do.
DANA: You'll also hear from Jacob. He's a character we made up, based on a situation from this training.
DANA: I'm Dana.
RAY: And I'm Ray. I've worked in safety my whole career, at a few different companies.
DANA: Let's get into it.

**What went wrong**
- It opens on an image with no payoff. Lifting the bag isn't the moment; what happens next is.
- Dana talks for six lines straight. That's a monologue, not a conversation.
- It mentions a character. Characters appear only in their own part.
- Ray volunteers his background. He shares it only when asked, or as an aside that supports a point.

**Better**

DANA: Today we're talking about sharps: a hazard you can walk right past, why a container does more than a pair of gloves, and what most people on the floor actually do.
DANA: I'm Dana.
RAY: And I'm Ray.
DANA: Let's get into it.

**Approved (generated, then kept as written)**

DANA: Our topic today is sharps. The hazards that are easy to miss, why a container beats gloves, and what people on the floor really do.
DANA: I'm Dana.
RAY: And I'm Ray.
DANA: Here we go.

From review of the first topic part.

| RAY: Most of my working life. Plants, warehouses, a couple of labs. | RAY: Uhh, pretty much my entire working life. I've worked in plants, warehouses, and even a couple of labs. | Answers are full spoken sentences, with a little improvisation. |

| DANA: So what does the shortcut look like? | DANA: We talked a bit before the show and you mentioned shortcuts. So what does a shortcut look like? | Never refer to something the listener hasn't heard yet. |
| RAY: The sharps container is at the far end of the line. So the old blade goes down next to your tools. You have an old blade in your hand, but you don't want to walk that far, so you set it down next to your tools and tell yourself, "I'll walk it over after this." | Frame the scenario, then let it flow as one connected thought. |

## What not to copy

These were caught in review. Don't repeat them.

- **"Hundreds of injuries related to it every year"** is not in the claims ledger. Never state a figure the ledger doesn't hold.
- **The example list** (nails, knives, metal) must be checked against the source content before it's used.
- **The open promises "what they are,"** which is the skippable blade topic's job. An open only promises what every learner hears.
- **"Any final thoughts?" and "Solid advice, Ray"** are fine once per listen, not in every part.
