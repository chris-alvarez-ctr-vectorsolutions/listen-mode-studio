## 7. Craft rules

- **The open** is a cold open: the sharpest line in content every learner hears (a line from a story, a striking detail), then the hosts' names and one short line to start. It is not an agenda read-out and does not list what's coming. Under 60 words. A quote is framed as a quote ("One person told us: ..."), never spoken in a host's first person, unless the character speaks it in their own voice and that story's part plays in every listen. No "you'll hear who said that" teasers. Quoted story lines are allowed here only when that story's part plays in every listen. No host backgrounds.
- **The host intro** exists only when the cast guide gives the expert a background (the text under the expert's name). With no background there is no host intro: the open ends on its short line to start, and the first topic part follows. Never invent a background, a role question or a career for the expert. When there is a background, the host intro follows the open in the same asset, with no sting between them, so it plays for every learner. The expert is a co-host who has already introduced himself, so the host never introduces him as a guest ("you're the practitioner on the show"). The host says the expert's name and states one specific fact taken from the background text in her own words, as something she knows about him, and asks one short question that invites a reaction or a small story from him (not a request to recite his career). The expert answers in one or two plain sentences, in his own words and in the first person: he answers her question, then gives a view or a feeling that builds on that background (what it lets him picture or notice). He doesn't parrot her sentence back or list the background the way she did, and he adds no career fact, role, employer or incident that isn't in the background text, and the host closes by tying that experience to the subject of the whole episode, in one line in her own words (what his experience means for today's subject), never a generic hand-off such as "let's get going" or "let's start." The tie names the episode's subject, not any topic part. 40 to 80 words. It names no topic part and promises nothing, because any topic part can be skipped; the first part's own signpost starts the content. The open's closing line is a plain hand-off that promises nothing, never "a quick hello" or "first, ...". Characterization only: it never carries a fact, and the background is not repeated in other parts except as a short aside that backs up a point.
- **Every setup needs its payoff in the same part.** If a line sets up a moment (a hand reaching for something, a step about to be skipped), the part must deliver what happens next: the consequence, the near miss, the choice. Never tell the listener to hold onto a picture.
- **No speaker has more than two lines in a row.** The exception is a character telling their own story.
- At least one concrete, visual detail per part (the specific thing in the specific place, not an abstract label).
- Plain words, contractions, few idioms. Many listeners speak English as a second language.
- **Answers sound like speech.** Mostly full spoken sentences, but vary the rhythm the way people do: a short fragment, a trailing thought or a quick restart is fine in an answer when a person would say it that way ("Twenty years. Different kinds of places."). Never a bare list of notes, and never telegraphic fragments with no connective. A short reaction rides at the front of the next line, never its own turn.
- **Every question connects to the answer before it.** Dana builds on what Ray just said ("So if you walked into a new company today...") instead of jumping to a new question cold.
- **Nothing is referred to before it's introduced.** Never "the rule" until someone has said which rule.
- **At most one interruption per part, in the conversational parts only.** When a speaker jumps in, mark it in the text: a dash ending the interrupted line and a dash starting the interrupting one ("So the part people forget is—" / "—the label, right."). Use it only where a real listener would already know where the line is going. Never in a part about harm, never inside a character's own story, and never in a way that cuts off a ledger claim. The interrupted line must still make sense without its ending.
- **Scenarios are framed and flow.** Ray sets up a hypothetical out loud ("Let's say...") and links the steps with "but," "so" and "and," in one or two sentences, rather than a string of short separate statements.
- At most one light moment per part. Humor comes from the situation, not quips.
- Each part uses one way in and one way out from the lists below. None repeats within a listen. These describe moves; write fresh wording every time.

**Ways in**
- Dana asks the question a listener would ask, aimed at the expert ("you"), not at the room, without announcing it.
- Dana says the narrow thing she would have assumed, then asks what else is true.
- Ray describes something he saw on the job, with its outcome.
- Dana admits she's made the same mistake herself.
- A surprising fact, from the claims ledger only.
- A plain one-line setup of the topic.
- The tease: Ray hints, Dana asks, Ray names it.

**Ways out**
- Ray's rule of thumb, in his own words. Once per listen.
- Dana says what she'd do differently now.
- A callback to the moment the part opened with.
- A short acknowledgment that closes the part cleanly.
- A question left with the listener.

## 8. Length

| Asset | Words | About |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open | 25 to 60 | 10 to 25 seconds |
| Host intro (same asset as the open) | 40 to 80 | 15 to 30 seconds |
| Topic part (Base, Test-up, Reinforced or single) | 200 to 280 | 1:30 to 2:00 |
| Close | 20 to 50 | 10 to 20 seconds |

Runtime = words / 140, plus holds, plus about 3 seconds per sting.

## 9. Render conventions (ElevenLabs)

- Model: Eleven v4 (same tag handling as v3). Start speed at 0.95.
- Render text holds spoken words, bracketed delivery tags and punctuation only. SFX, beds, holds and stings live on the cue sheet.
- Speaker labels (DANA:, RAY:) map lines to voices. Send only the text after the colon.
- Tags: about four to eight per part, only where the meaning or emotion changes, never on consecutive lines. Place a tag just before the line it shapes, or just after a line as a reaction ([sighs], [laughs]). Keep to each voice's palette. Never [quietly], [softly] or a variant on a host, expert or narrator. A tag may be two short descriptive words in that voice's register ([warm, amused], [dry, deadpan]); never staging or sound tags ([music], [standing]).
  - Ray: [dry] [matter-of-fact] [thoughtful] [warmly] [laughs] [amused] [reassuring] [sighs] [patient] [firmly] [concerned] [wry] [slowly] [chuckles] [exhales] [clears throat]
  - Dana: [playful] [skeptical] [curious] [surprised] [sighs] [laughs] [amused] [warmly] [jumping in] [realizing] [impressed] [wry] [unsure] [relieved] [mock-serious] [thinking out loud] [exhales] [clears throat]
  - Characters: [tired] [flatly] [quietly] [sighs] [nervously]
- Emphasis: CAPS on one or two words per part. No SSML.
- Short pauses: an ellipsis, a dash or punctuation (never [pause] or SSML break tags). Anything 2 seconds or longer is a hold on the cue sheet.
- Spell out as spoken: numbers ("more than nine out of ten"), initialisms (H-I-V, P-P-E, U-S).
- Split each part into render segments at every hold.
