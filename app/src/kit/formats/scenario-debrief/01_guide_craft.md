## 7. Craft rules

- **The open** is a cold open: the sharpest line in content every learner hears (a line from a story, a striking detail), then the hosts' names and one short line to start. It is not an agenda read-out and does not list what's coming. Under 60 words. Quoted story lines are allowed here only when that story's part plays in every listen. No host backgrounds.
- **The host intro** exists only when the cast guide gives the expert a background (the text under the expert's name). With no background there is no host intro: the open ends on its short line to start, and the first topic part follows. Never invent a background, a role question or a career for the expert. When there is a background, the host intro follows the open in the same asset, with no sting between them, so it plays for every learner. The expert is a co-host who has already introduced himself, so the host never introduces him as a guest ("you're the practitioner on the show"). The host says the expert's name and states one specific fact taken from the background text in the host's own words, as something she knows about him, and asks a short question that fits it. The expert answers in two or three sentences in his own words, using that background and nothing more, and the host says one short line to move on. 40 to 80 words. It names no topic part and promises nothing, because any topic part can be skipped; the first part's own signpost starts the content. The open's closing line is a plain hand-off that promises nothing, never "a quick hello" or "first, ...". Characterization only: it never carries a fact, and the background is not repeated in other parts except as a short aside that backs up a point.
- **Signpost first.** Before any story, Dana's first line tells the listener a story is starting and what it's about in plain words, conversationally and never as a label like "New topic:" ("Here's a story about one used needle." works because it follows an announcement of the topic, not because it is cold). Never open a part with a story line that assumes the listener knew one was coming.
- **Hand off to every voice.** When a character will speak, Dana introduces them first, by name and role, and says the listener is about to hear from them ("Let's hear it from [name], who [does their role]."). If the story has two people, each gets their own introduction and hand-off before their first line. A person Dana tells about in the third person (the officer) is never followed by a different, unintroduced speaker.
- **Start wide, then narrow.** The part opens on the big picture, then one place and one person. Dana states the plan, then the moment it fell apart, with a time marker ("It was twenty minutes before the end of the shift.").
- **The character tells it in the first person when the story has more than a few lines.** Dana only compresses time and place. The character's lines are short, concrete sentences, and the story is a connected run of them. If the story is short (about 80 words or fewer), Ray or Dana may tell it in the third person instead, and no character voice is needed.
- **The character is never interviewed.** The character's account is a recording, not a live guest. Nobody asks the character a question, speaks to the character, or waits for an answer, and the character never answers anyone. After the account, the hosts react to it in the third person, briefly. If the listener needs the other side of the story, Dana simply tells it ("Here's what happened on the other end."). She does not ask the character for it.
- **The explanation arrives inside the story, at the moment it's needed.** When the character reaches the moment that matters, Ray steps in with what he'd notice and why he'd act, in one or two plain sentences, tied to the cue that triggered it. Then the story goes on. Never save it for a summary.
- **Small physical details carry the emotion.** A hand, a sound, a box left half open. Name what happened. Never name the feeling for the listener.
- **One plain content warning before any graphic detail.** One sentence, said by Dana, with no drama.
- **End on a second person's reaction, not a moral, and never on a summary by Dana or Ray.** The last words belong to a person in the story (a coworker quoted by the character) or to Ray, in their own words. Nobody states the lesson.
- **Every setup needs its payoff in the same part.** If a line sets up a moment, the part delivers what happens next. Never tell the listener to hold onto a picture.
- **The fact rule holds inside a story.** Every technical detail (a number, a symptom, a step in the response) comes from the claims ledger. Anything the story invents is characterization.
- **If the debrief is its own part,** open it with one line restating the moment in plain words, so it stands alone if the story part is skipped. Keep Ray's reasoning tied to the cue.
- **No speaker has more than two lines in a row.** The exception is the character telling his own story.
- Plain words, contractions, few idioms. Many listeners speak English as a second language.
- **Answers sound like speech.** Mostly full spoken sentences, but vary the rhythm the way people do: a short fragment, a trailing thought or a quick restart is fine in an answer when a person would say it that way ("Twenty years. Different kinds of places."). Never a bare list of notes, and never telegraphic fragments with no connective. A short reaction rides at the front of the next line, never its own turn.
- **Every question from Dana connects to what was just said.**
- **Nothing is referred to before it's introduced.**
- **At most one interruption per part, in the conversational parts only.** When a speaker jumps in, mark it in the text: a dash ending the interrupted line and a dash starting the interrupting one ("So the part people forget is—" / "—the label, right."). Use it only where a real listener would already know where the line is going. Never in a part about harm, never inside a character's own story, and never in a way that cuts off a ledger claim. The interrupted line must still make sense without its ending.
- At most one light moment per part, and none in a part about harm. Humor comes from the situation, not quips.
- Each part uses one way in and one way out from the lists below. None repeats within a listen. These describe moves; write fresh wording every time.

**Ways in**
- Dana names the big picture, then narrows to one person.
- Dana states the plan, then the moment it fell apart.
- The character starts mid-moment, with where he was standing.
- Dana asks the plain question the story answers.
- A surprising fact, from the claims ledger only.

**Ways out**
- A second person's reaction, in their own words.
- Ray's rule of thumb, in his own words. Once per listen.
- A callback to the moment the part opened with.
- A short acknowledgment that closes the part cleanly.
- A question left with the listener.

## 8. Length

| Asset | Words | About |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open | 25 to 60 | 10 to 25 seconds |
| Host intro (same asset as the open) | 40 to 80 | 15 to 30 seconds |
| Topic part (Base, Test-up or single) | 200 to 280 | 1:30 to 2:00 |
| Close | 20 to 50 | 10 to 20 seconds |

Runtime = words / 140, plus holds, plus about 3 seconds per sting.

## 9. Render conventions (ElevenLabs)

- Model: Eleven v4 (same tag handling as v3). Start speed at 0.95.
- Render text holds spoken words, bracketed delivery tags and punctuation only. SFX, beds, holds and stings live on the cue sheet.
- Speaker labels (DANA:, JACOB:, RAY:) map lines to voices. Send only the text after the colon.
- Tags: about four to eight per part, only where the meaning or emotion changes, never on consecutive lines. Place a tag just before the line it shapes, or just after a line as a reaction ([sighs], [laughs]). Keep to each voice's palette. A tag may be two short descriptive words in that voice's register ([warm, amused], [dry, deadpan]); never staging or sound tags ([music], [standing]).
  - Ray: [dry] [matter-of-fact] [thoughtful] [warmly] [laughs] [quietly] [amused] [reassuring] [sighs]
  - Dana: [playful] [skeptical] [curious] [surprised] [sighs] [laughs] [quietly] [amused] [warmly] [jumping in]
  - Jacob: [tired] [flatly] [quietly] [sighs] [nervously]
- Emphasis: CAPS on one or two words per part. No SSML.
- Short pauses: an ellipsis, a dash or punctuation (never [pause] or SSML break tags). Anything 2 seconds or longer is a hold on the cue sheet.
- Spell out as spoken: numbers ("more than nine out of ten"), initialisms (H-I-V, P-P-E, U-S).
- Split each part into render segments at every hold and at the ending.
