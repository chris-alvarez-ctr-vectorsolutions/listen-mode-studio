## 3. Part types and pathways

| Label | What it is | How it plays |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base | The default version of a topic | Plays unless swapped or removed |
| Test-up | A harder scene with the same facts | **Replaces** Base for learners who prove the basics in the pre-check. Compliance-locked Know topics test up instead of out. |
| Reinforced | A fuller version of a Feel topic, with the same facts and more weight | **Replaces** Base for learners who score 3 or lower on the 1 to 5 agreement item in the pre-check (4 and 5 are high) |
| Single version | A topic with one version, such as a Feel/Value topic that is only asked about once per course | Always plays |

Routing is per objective, from the pre-check:
- **Know, not locked:** a correct pre-check answer tests the learner out. The topic is removed from their listen.
- **Know, compliance-locked:** never tests out. A correct pre-check answer plays the Test-up instead of the Base.
- **Feel:** never removes content. A low score plays the Reinforced version instead of the Base.
- **Do:** not in the audio. The simulation runs for every learner.

The pathway table in each module fixes which versions exist, so don't add, drop or rename any. Remedial content and review cards are not audio: a separate interactive step delivers them after the checks, so never write them.

**The master script** is the Full listen: every topic's Base (or single) version in order. Every other listen is the master with a part removed or swapped. A listen with fewer than three chapters isn't built; the learner goes to the article instead.

## 4. Modular rules

1. Every part stands alone. No references to other parts: no "again," "this time," "like we said."
2. Topic order is fixed. Each position always covers the same subject.
3. The open may only promise content from positions that play in every listen. It never promises a topic that can test out. In formats with a background expert, and only when the cast gives the expert a background, the host intro follows the open in the same asset. It plays for every learner, names no topic part, and is where the expert's background is given.
4. No bridges inside a part, but a signpost. (Short bridges between parts are written separately in the Seams stage, as their own assets, and a part must also work with none.) Each part's first exchange moves the listener into the topic the way a person would talk, not with a label. The host brings it up conversationally, in a different way for each part. Choose from these moves and write fresh wording every time: turn to a co-speaker with a question that leads into the topic; put a small everyday situation in front of the listener and ask what they would do; ask the co-speaker a plain "what is" or "why does" question; state the plain stake in one sentence; or, in a solo format, speak straight to the listener. Addressing a co-speaker by name is allowed at most once per listen, and never as a formula such as "[name], let's talk about…". It names the topic in plain words and says what kind of thing comes next (a story, a question, a case). Never "New topic:" or "Next up:". It works whatever played before it and never drops the listener cold into the content. No reference to another part.
4a. Introduce every voice before it speaks. The first time a voice other than the host is heard in a part, the host names that person and their role in one plain line and hands off to them ("Here's [name], who [does their role]."). Never narrate a person in the third person and then cut to a different voice with no hand-off. A person the host only talks about is not a voice, and the listener should be able to tell the difference.
5. Test-up and Reinforced versions stand alone. The learner never heard the Base, and the listen has no memory of it.
6. One shared anchor. The module's plan names one setting or object (a property tray, a booking counter). Every part, including Test-up and Reinforced versions, uses it concretely, so parts hold together without bridges. Using the anchor is not a reference to another part.
7. The open starts on the sharpest line in content that plays in every listen (a quote from a story, a striking detail), then gives the names. It is not an agenda read-out. The close, written in the Seams stage, ends on a reflection question or a callback to the last part the learner heard.

## 5. Fact rules (these override everything else)

- Facts come only from the signed claims ledger. Rephrase, round down or simplify freely. Never add to a claim, strengthen it, generalize it, or combine two into a new one.
- Examples (like a list of items that fall under a rule) must appear in the source content or the ledger.
- A line that needs a missing fact: write around it and list the gap. A new fact goes to the SME, not into the script.
- Flagged claims are used only as the ledger allows, marked, and never resolved in the script.
- Characters' and hosts' personal moments never carry a fact, statistic, injury, exposure or outcome.

## 6. Learning rules

- Each part serves one objective and carries that objective's required claims.
- Know parts restate the core rule once, near the end.
- Feel parts change what the listener believes through story or evidence. They don't instruct.
- Perceive (norm) parts show what people do. They never tell the listener what to do.
- A judgment hold is optional: at most one per Know part, and it is only ever a question put to the listener. The host (or narrator) steps outside the conversation, names the listener as the audience, and asks it directly in plain words ("Before we hear the answer, let's ask our audience... what would you do?"). The cue sheet then holds 3 seconds. Right after the hold, the host (or narrator) brings the answerer back in with a short line that hands over the floor ("Okay, [name], let's hear your answer now.", using the answerer's name), and only then does the answer follow (from the expert, or from the narrator). The hand-back line opens the segment after the hold and is never skipped. Never put a hold after a question one speaker asks another ("So what do they do?"): the other speaker just answers, with no silence. If the question isn't really for the listener, there is no hold.

## 6a. Conversation feel (all formats with two or more voices)

Write like two people talking, not a host feeding an expert. These are the rules that matter most for how it sounds.

- **The host does more than ask.** She reacts, sets up a scenario in her own words, says what she would do differently now, and sometimes voices a common wrong assumption. She is curious, never a straight man. Around a third of her turns should be something other than a question.
- **Every question is handed to someone.** When the host voices a question on the listener's behalf, she aims it at the expert in the same turn, with a plain "you" or an invitation ("what do you tell people?", "how bad is it, in your experience?"), never a general question to the room followed by "let's see". The expert's turn answers what he was just asked. He never starts talking because the host paused. Using his name for this still counts toward the once-per-listen limit, so mostly use "you".
- **The host does not echo the expert.** After the expert says a rule or a step, the host's next turn moves on (a reaction, a scenario, a new question). She never restates it, and a part does not state its key point more than the one permitted restatement.
- **A wrong assumption is corrected at once.** The host may say what "people often assume" or what she would have done, but only when the claims ledger backs the correct version, and the expert corrects it in full in his very next turn. She never states a wrong fact as settled, and a wrong belief is never left standing across a turn. Vary how she voices it ("I'd have..." at most once per listen), and the same goes for how she hands a question to the listener.
- **A reaction rides on the next line.** A one-word reaction ("Right." "Huh.") is never its own turn. Attach it to the front of the same speaker's next line ("Right. So where does the next one go?"). It carries a real reaction, and it is not used to acknowledge every turn.
- **Vary turn length.** Mix fragments, short turns and longer ones. Turns of the same size, one after another, sound read out.
- **Casual markers are rare.** "Honestly," "you know," "look," "pretty much," "funny enough" appear at most once per part, never on consecutive turns, never as the first word of a turn, and a different one each time in a listen. Most parts have none. Never "uhh."
- **No repeated frames.** Any question-and-answer pattern ("Even X?" / "Even X.", a tease with a one-word reveal, a "what's the one thing" question) is used once per listen.
- **No restating.** A point is not said twice in the same turn or the next one. A figure is given in one form for the whole listen (not "92.7 percent" and later "nine out of ten"). A Know part restates its core rule once, in the expert's words, and the host does not echo it.
- **Stories land before facts.** No fact, list or statistic inside a character's story. The story finishes, then the expert gives the facts.
- **No staging or politeness fillers.** No "before the show" or "off mic" setups, no hosts thanking each other for explaining, and no canned personality lines ("I like a good pair of gloves as much as anyone"). An aside stays only when it backs up a point.
- **One question at a time.** The host never stacks questions, and the expert doesn't answer a list in order.
- **Vary the shape of each part.** Don't repeat scenario, host question, expert paragraph, closing rule line across parts. Some parts end without a tidy rule line.
- **No stated lesson in Feel parts or stories.** Neither voice sums up the moral.
- **The expert sounds like a person, not a paragraph.** He may give a short aside from experience or concede a small point of wording. Asides never carry a fact. No recited procedure in a list voice.
- **CAPS** only on a word the speaker truly stresses, once or twice per part.
